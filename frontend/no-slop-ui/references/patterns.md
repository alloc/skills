# Generic UI Pattern Diagnostics

Judge patterns by their effect on the product's purpose and visual language. Rounded navigation, pills, gradients, uppercase labels, and spring motion can all be appropriate. Their familiarity alone is not a reason to remove them.

## Competing Emphasis

Look for treatments that make the important content or action harder to find: a large hero before routine work, equally prominent metrics with different importance, or effects that overpower labels and state. Adjust relative emphasis around the user's decisions. Expressive typography, imagery, color, and motion can establish identity or atmosphere when the task remains clear.

## Meaningless Grouping

Look for containers and regions that imply relationships the content does not have. Examples include wrapping every section in a card or adding an activity rail merely to balance a layout. Group by meaningful relationships; use spacing, headings, surfaces, or dividers as the content requires. Consistent rounding or elevation is not itself a defect.

## Unclear Interaction

Check whether users can distinguish actions, filters, tags, and status indicators, regardless of their shapes. Labels and feedback should explain what a control does and what happened.

When an icon's meaning is ambiguous to the intended audience, provide a visible label. Give controls appropriate accessible names as well; an accessible name alone does not resolve ambiguity for sighted users.

Do not rely on color alone for status or selection. Pair it with text, shape, iconography, position, or another suitable cue.

## Decoration That Impedes the Task

Look for effects that weaken contrast, space that separates related content or pushes necessary actions out of reach, and transitions that delay repeated work. Reduce the interference while preserving useful brand expression. Motion should remain responsive and interruptible where appropriate, honor accessibility preferences, and preserve state clarity when reduced.

## Generic or Misleading Content

Replace vague promotional copy and empty urgency with concrete objects, actions, and states. Badges and secondary content should convey information users can interpret.

Charts should answer a meaningful question. In prototypes, clearly identified sample data is appropriate: use plausible relationships and representative conditions without presenting invented metrics as factual claims. In production, use real data or an honest empty or unavailable state.

## Layout That Only Works for Ideal Content

Inspect sparse, dense, long, missing, and erroneous content. Check whether smaller layouts preserve task priority rather than merely stacking every region. Match density to the work and reading context; spaciousness and compactness are both valid when they support use.

Recommend a change when you can explain the resulting improvement in comprehension, interaction, or product expression. Keep coherent, intentional choices even when they resemble a common generated interface.
